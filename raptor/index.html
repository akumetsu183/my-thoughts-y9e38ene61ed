Raptor 1 → 4: rated vs reliable

Every engine in this family has two thrust numbers: what it makes on the test stand, and what it holds in flight. For Raptor 3 the gap is 50 tons per engine — 1,650 tons across the booster. That gap is the whole reason Raptor 4 exists.

“We can only reliably get to around 230 t-F with Raptor 3.” Elon Musk, 26 Oct 2026 — three hours after announcing Raptor 4 at 300 tons

Raptor 3's performance card says 280 tf. Musk's own caveat says the engine reliably reaches about 230. Both are true, and the difference between them is not a rounding error — it is a quarter of the booster's total thrust, and it lands directly on payload.

The booster carries 33 engines, so a 50 tf shortfall per engine removes about 1,650 tf — roughly half a Saturn V — from what Starship actually lifts with. Raptor 4 at 300 tf, if it holds reliably, would put the booster back to 9,900 tf.

02 What the derate costs Starship

Block 3's published payload assumes a booster it does not have.

If Raptor 3 ran at its 280 tf card figure, Block 3 would carry roughly 111 t reusable instead of 100 t — an 11 t penalty paid every flight because the engine runs at 230. That is the concrete price of the reliability gap, and it is what Raptor 4 is meant to recover and then some.

Bar length is the heaviest payload actually deployed, as a share of Block 3's paper reusable figure. Block 3 has flown a little over half of its design number, once.

Where Block 4 lands

Raptor 4 takes the booster from 7,590 tf reliable to 9,900 tf — a 30% thrust increase, on a ship with 44% more propellant. Scaling Block 3's ratio and converting half the thrust gain into payload gives 166 t reusable, with a plausible band of 141–191 t. Expendable, where the ship is thrown away and the tanks burned dry, lands near 662 t.

How these numbers were made

  1. The anchor is the reliable engine. Block 3's published 100 t reusable is the design figure for a 230 tf booster — the engine that flies. It is not a 280 tf booster.
  2. One scaling rule. Reusable payload tracks ship propellant at 0.0625 t per tonne; expendable at 0.25 t per tonne. Both ratios read straight off Block 3.
  3. Thrust correction. Ship propellant 1,600 → 2,300 t alone gives 144 t. Raptor 4's +30% reliable thrust is applied at half strength — the booster is thrust-limited off the pad, but not everything converts.
  4. Result. 166 t reusable, 662 t expendable, each with a ±15% band because the structural fraction is the real unknown.

Why not run Tsiolkovsky directly? Because it doesn't close on public data. Feed it the published dry masses and the stack either runs out of delta-v before orbit or produces several hundred tonnes of imaginary payload, depending on what you assume for landing reserves and unlisted structure. Anchoring on a real flown figure and scaling one variable is duller and far more defensible — and it lets the page show you exactly where the guess starts.

04 The reality check

Worth separating two things that get conflated in every Starship argument: what a block is designed to lift, and what it has actually lifted. The heaviest payload Starship has deployed to orbit is 26 Starlink V3 satellites — about 52 t — on Flight 14, and that was Block 3's first operational flight.

That is not a scandal; it is what a flight-test campaign looks like. Block 1 and Block 2 never flew anything near their design numbers either, and Block 3 has flown three times. But it does mean the honest way to read “100 t reusable” is as a ceiling demonstrated to about half, once, on a vehicle still being debugged. Block 3 is sometimes quoted at 200 t reusable; nothing flown supports that, and the 100 t card figure is already a stretch of the evidence.

05 What is still speculation

Raptor 4's reliability. 300 tf is a target, not a flight rating. Raptor 3's card said 280 and delivered 230 reliably, so a sensible reader applies the same suspicion here — which is exactly why Block 4's 166 t is a projection with a band, not a promise.

The mass. “Hopefully about the same as Raptor 3” is Musk's phrasing. 1,550 kg is an assumption anywhere in the 1,500–1,650 kg range. The specific impulse. Not announced. Isp is the multiplier on any thrust gain — if Raptor 4's improvement is thrust-only, payload moves much less than 30%.

None of this is a reason to doubt the trajectory. 185 to 300 tf in seven years, with mass falling the whole way, is an extraordinary record. It is a reason to keep the reliable number and the card number apart — which is the entire point of this page.
