Raptor 4's 300 tf is a stated target rather than a derated flight rating, so it is carried as both rated and reliable. Its mass follows Musk's "roughly Raptor 3" and carries uncertainty.

What the derate costs

Raptor 3 is rated at 280 tf and reliably reaches about 230. Across 33 engines that is 1,650 tf of thrust the booster does not get to use.

Against a Saturn V's 3,400 tf, the lost thrust alone is nearly half a Saturn V.

Isp across generations

Vacuum Isp has not moved: 350, 347, 350 s across Raptor 1, 2 and 3. The sea-level figure rose only because the sea-level/vacuum gap closed — nozzle expansion work, not combustion.

These are calibrated, and the calibration is the honest part. The equation alone gave Block 3 159 t reusable, against a published 100 t and a real-world expectation nearer 70 t. The gap is ship dry mass: the published 85–155 t figures describe the steel structure only, and exclude heat shield, flaps, landing hardware and the propellant that can never be burned. Adding those puts Block 3 at 199 t dry. Fitted to 70 t there, the model puts Block 1 at 10 t — near its published 15 t — and Block 4 at 120 t, well below its 200 t claim.

Expendable is not simply "reusable minus the landing burn". An expendable ship also sheds the heat shield, flaps, actuators and landing gear — about 35 t of dry mass it never has to lift. On Block 3 the 85 t gap splits into roughly 53 t from giving up the 600 m/s landing reserve and 32 t from dropping the re-entry hardware, with the mass ratio compounding both.

Ship dry mass is an estimate: tank structure scales roughly with length at fixed diameter, plus 4.6 t for three more RVac.

What it buys

Block 4's reusable payload is 120 t, against Block 3's 70 t — a 71% gain for 60% more propellant. Part of that comes from residual propellant scaling with tank area rather than volume: a longer tank is structurally more efficient, so Block 4 gains more than its extra tankage alone would suggest.

Block 4's expendable figure is 225 t — which brackets SpaceX's 200 t claim closely enough that the published number is most likely an expendable one. Reusable is 120 t, and rises to ~141 t if the ship structure comes in at 135 t rather than 155 t. Above 100 t reusable is solid; 150 t is possible; 200 t reusable is not.

Six vacuum engines, not three

Going from three RVac to six does not change the delta-v arithmetic — specific impulse is per-engine and the mass flow simply doubles. What it changes is thrust: the ship's vacuum acceleration roughly doubles, which shortens the insertion burn, cuts gravity loss, and gives the vehicle margin to complete the burn on fewer engines if one fails. It also makes the ship a more capable tanker, since refuelling transfers scale with how quickly propellant can be moved.

The 60% propellant increase is the bigger lever, and it is not about payload. At its reusable limit a Block 3 ship reaches orbit with only about 47 t of propellant left — enough for the landing burn and little else. Block 4 arrives with roughly 66 t. Neither is a tanker. Orbital refuelling needs a ship that launches with little or no payload and keeps most of its tank, which is why the tanker variant exists as a separate vehicle rather than a Block 4 with cargo aboard.

05 The model

Two equations, published masses, and an honest account of what they cannot tell you.

  1. Booster burn. Isp_eff·g₀·ln(m₀/m₁), with the ship still fully fuelled at hot-staging and the booster keeping 10% of its propellant to fly back and land. Mean Isp is weighted 65/35 between sea level and vacuum.
  2. Ship burn. Isp_vac·g₀·ln((dry+prop+payload)/(dry+payload)). The vacuum engine's 378 s carries the ship from staging to orbit.
  3. Delta-v to LEO is 9,400 m/s, not 7,800. Orbital velocity alone is 7,800; gravity, drag and steering losses are worth ~1,600 more. Using the bare orbital figure was the single largest error in the earlier arithmetic.
  4. Ship dry mass is 1.2–1.3× the published structure, plus 4% residual propellant. Published dry masses describe the steel only. Heat shield, flaps, landing hardware and unusable propellant are all real mass that has to be lifted.
  5. Reusable means 600 m/s held back. Deorbit plus the landing burn after the flip. Entry is aerobraking and costs no propellant, so the reserve is small next to the ~9,400 m/s of ascent — but it is not zero.

The weak points are the fitted constants, and they should be read as assumptions. Three are anchored rather than derived: the ship hardware uplift, the 600 m/s landing reserve, and the 35 t of re-entry hardware an expendable ship drops. Block 1 and Block 3 are fitted to 10 t and 70 t reusable — judgement calls, not published figures — and Block 4 follows the maturity trend without being tuned at all. What supports the result is that Block 4 then lands at 120 t reusable and 225 t expendable, bracketing SpaceX's 200 t claim closely enough that the published figure looks like an expendable number. If the Block 4 ship structure comes in at 135 t rather than 155 t, reusable rises to about 141 t.
