Raptor 1 → 4: 185 to 300 tons

Four generations of the same engine, and the one number that moved every time: thrust went +62% while the engine got 25% lighter. In October 2026 Musk put Raptor 4 at 300 tons of sea-level thrust at roughly Raptor 3's mass. Here is what that actually buys Starship.

02 Where the gains came from

Thrust up, mass down. That second half is the part most summaries skip.

Raptor 1 was a development engine — heavy plumbing, external heat shielding, a lot of vehicle-side hardware that existed only to keep the engine alive through reentry. Raptor 2 simplified the manufacturing and cut 450 kg. Raptor 3 folded its sensors, controller and cooling into the engine itself and deleted the shielding entirely, which is why it dropped another ~105 kg while gaining 50 tf.

Thrust-to-weight is where it shows: 89 → 141 → 184 → ~194 kgf per kg. Every point of that is mass the booster no longer has to lift. Raptor 4's whole premise is that the same-mass trick keeps working one more time — no new architecture, just a bigger margin.

03 What 300 tons does to the stack

33 engines on Super Heavy. The arithmetic is the easy part.

At 300 tf, a 33-engine Super Heavy makes 9,900 tf — just under three Saturn Vs, chained together. But liftoff thrust alone is the wrong headline now. Thrust-to-weight on the whole stack is what decides how much propellant is left for payload, and Block 4's extra engines only pay off if the growth is spent on propellant, not on structure.

04 The payload estimate

Reusable mode keeps the ship. Expendable mode throws it away — and that is a very different rocket.

How these numbers were made

  1. Anchors, not derivation. Block 3 has published figures for both modes: ~200 t reusable and ~400 t expendable. Those are the only two hard points.
  2. One scaling rule. Reusable payload tracks ship propellant at 0.125 t per tonne; expendable at 0.25 t per tonne. Both ratios are read straight off Block 3.
  3. Apply to Block 4. Ship propellant 1,600 → 2,300 t (+44%). That gives 288 t reusable and 575 t expendable.
  4. Sanity check. Wikipedia lists Block 4 as 200 t (est.), and Musk has said V4 should exceed 200 t reusable. My 288 t sits above both — so the page shows a band, not a single number.

Why not just run Tsiolkovsky? Because it doesn't close. Try it with the public dry masses and the stack runs out of delta-v long before orbit — the published mass figures are rounded, the non-propellant structure isn't itemised, and the landing reserves are never stated. A rocket equation built on those inputs produces confident nonsense (several hundred tonnes over, or a negative payload, depending which reserves you assume). The scaling above is less satisfying and far more honest: it is anchored on two real numbers and shows you exactly how far the guess extends.

The band, stated plainly

If Block 4's structure grows faster than its tanks, reusable payload lands near 207 t — Block 2's achieved ratio of 0.09 t per tonne of ship prop. If it holds Block 3's efficiency, it lands near 334 t. The central 288 t assumes no improvement and no regression in the structural fraction, which is the least interesting assumption and the only defensible one.

Expendable is where the number gets silly, and it's worth being clear why. Throwing the ship away means you no longer carry the heat shield, the flaps, the landing propellant or the return reserve — and you get to burn the last of the tanks dry on the way up. The payload roughly doubles. Block 4 at 575 t expendable is more than every rocket ever flown in a single decade, in one launch — which is precisely why nobody plans to operate that way. It's a capability, not a business model.

05 What is still speculation

Three things on this page are projected rather than reported, and they are the three that matter most for the V4 numbers:

The mass. "Roughly the same as Raptor 3" is Musk's phrasing. 1,550 kg is an assumption; anywhere 1,500–1,650 kg is consistent with what he said. The specific impulse. Not announced at all. I've assumed 353 s sea level, continuing Raptor 3's trajectory. If Raptor 4's gain is thrust-only, payload barely moves — Isp is the multipler. The chamber pressure. 300 tf at constant mass implies a meaningful pressure increase over Raptor 3, and that is the engineering claim the whole projection rests on.

None of that is a reason to distrust the engine — 185 to 300 tf in seven years is an extraordinary record. It's a reason to date the estimate: as of 26 October 2026, and no earlier.
