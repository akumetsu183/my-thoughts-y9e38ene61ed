Raptor 1 → 4: rated vs reliable

Every engine in this family has two thrust numbers: what it makes on the test stand, and what it holds in flight. For Raptor 3 the gap is 50 tons per engine — 1,650 tons across the booster. That gap is the whole reason Raptor 4 exists.

“We can only reliably get to around 230 t-F with Raptor 3.” Elon Musk, 26 Oct 2026 — three hours after announcing Raptor 4 at 300 tons

Raptor 3's performance card says 280 tf. Musk's own caveat says the engine reliably reaches about 230. Both are true, and the difference between them is not a rounding error — it is a quarter of the booster's total thrust, and it lands directly on payload.

The booster carries 33 engines, so a 50 tf shortfall per engine removes about 1,650 tf — roughly half a Saturn V — from what Starship actually lifts with. Raptor 4 at 300 tf, if it holds reliably, would put the booster back to 9,900 tf.

02 What the derate costs Starship

Block 3's published payload assumes a booster it does not have.

If Raptor 3 ran at its 280 tf card figure, Block 3 would carry roughly 111 t reusable instead of 100 t — an 11 t penalty paid every flight because the engine runs at 230. That is the concrete price of the reliability gap, and it is what Raptor 4 is meant to recover and then some.

Why only Block 3 was ever 100 tonnes

The “100 t reusable” figure gets repeated for every Starship block, but solving the rocket equation for each one shows it only holds for Block 3. Blocks 1 and 2 come out well below it — 53 t and 65 t — because their ships carry less propellant on a proportionally heavier structure. Nothing in the maths was told to produce that; it falls out of the masses.

Block 4: a range, not a number

Block 4 is where the rocket equation stops being polite. Its ship carries 2,300 t of propellant on a 115 t dry ship — a 20:1 ratio against Block 3's 14.5:1 — so its payload is extraordinarily sensitive to two things nobody has published: Raptor 4's specific impulse, and what mission delta-v the vehicle is actually required to hit.

Read the top-left to bottom-right diagonal and you get the argument in one line: Block 4 is worth somewhere between 60 t and 280 t, and which end you land on depends on assumptions nobody outside SpaceX can check. If Raptor 4's Isp improves as expected and the mission budget stays as loose as Block 3's, it approaches 250 t. If the vehicle is asked to fly harder missions — which a heavier ship with more landing propellant plausibly would be — it lands near 100 t, which is where the honest guess sits.

The equation, and the one constant in it

  1. Two-stage Tsiolkovsky. Booster: Isp_sl·g₀·ln((dry+prop+above)/(dry+reserve+above)). Ship: Isp_vac·g₀·ln((dry+prop+payload)/(dry+reserve+payload)).
  2. One budget, calibrated once. Orbital velocity plus gravity, steering, drag and landing losses. Block 3's published 100 t is required to close at that budget, which sets it at 7,803 m/s.
  3. Everything else follows. Blocks 1, 2 and 4 use the same budget and the same equation. No per-block tuning, no scaling factors.
  4. Thrust does not enter. Payload depends on Isp, not thrust — so the Raptor 3 230/280 distinction belongs to the thrust story at the top of this page, not to the payload arithmetic. That is a common confusion worth naming.

The weak point is the masses, not the maths. Public dry masses are rounded, the non-propellant structure is never itemised, and landing reserves are inferred. A ±10% error in ship dry mass moves Block 3's answer by roughly ±15 t and Block 4's by far more, because a 20:1 propellant ratio is a lever. Treat the numbers as a well-built estimate, not a measurement — and treat Block 4's spread as the honest answer rather than a failure to compute one.

04 What is still speculation

Raptor 4's reliability. 300 tf is a target, not a flight rating. Raptor 3's card said 280 and delivered 230 reliably, so a sensible reader applies the same suspicion here — which is exactly why Block 4's 254 t is a projection with a band, not a promise.

The mass. “Hopefully about the same as Raptor 3” is Musk's phrasing. 1,550 kg is an assumption anywhere in the 1,500–1,650 kg range. The specific impulse. Not announced. Isp is the multiplier on any thrust gain — if Raptor 4's improvement is thrust-only, payload moves much less than 30%.

None of this is a reason to doubt the trajectory. 185 to 300 tf in seven years, with mass falling the whole way, is an extraordinary record. It is a reason to keep the reliable number and the card number apart — which is the entire point of this page.
