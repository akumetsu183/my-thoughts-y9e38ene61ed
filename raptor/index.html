Raptor 4's 300 tf is a stated target rather than a derated flight rating, so it is carried as both rated and reliable. Its mass follows Musk's "roughly Raptor 3" and carries some uncertainty.

What the derate costs

Raptor 3 is rated at 280 tf and reliably reaches about 230. Across 33 engines that is 1,650 tf of thrust the booster does not get to use.

Held against a Saturn V's 3,400 tf, the lost thrust alone is nearly half a Saturn V.

Isp across generations

Vacuum Isp has not moved: 350, 347, 350 s across Raptor 1, 2 and 3. The sea-level figure rose only because the sea-level/vacuum gap closed — nozzle expansion work, not combustion.

04 The model

Everything above comes from the two equations below and one number fitted to a published fact.

  1. Two-stage Tsiolkovsky. Booster: Isp_sl·g₀·ln((dry+prop+above)/(dry+reserve+above)). Ship: Isp_vac·g₀·ln((dry+prop+payload)/(dry+reserve+payload)).
  2. One calibrated constant. Orbital velocity plus gravity, steering, drag and landing losses. Block 3's published 100 t must close at that budget, which fixes it at 7,803 m/s.
  3. Everything else follows. Blocks 1, 2 and 4 use the same budget and the same equations. No per-block tuning, no scaling factors.
  4. Thrust does not enter. Payload depends on Isp, not thrust. The 230/280 tf distinction belongs to the thrust story at the top of this page, not to the payload arithmetic — a common confusion worth naming.
  5. Propellant density is not free. Subcooled LOX at 1,280 kg/m³ and methane at 450 kg/m³, mixed 3.6:1 by mass, give 914 kg/m³ bulk. Using warmer LOX puts the booster tanks above their own geometric volume.

The weak point is the masses, not the maths. Public dry masses are rounded, the non-propellant structure is never itemised, and landing reserves are inferred. A ±10% error in ship dry mass moves Block 3's answer by roughly ±15 t and Block 4's by more, because a 20:1 propellant ratio is a lever. Treat these numbers as a well-built estimate, not a measurement.
