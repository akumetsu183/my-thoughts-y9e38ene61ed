01 The four engines

Raptor 4's 300 tf is a stated target rather than a derated flight rating, so it is carried as both rated and reliable. Its mass follows Musk's "roughly Raptor 3" and carries uncertainty.

What the derate costs

Raptor 3 is rated at 280 tf and reliably reaches about 230. Across 33 engines that is 1,650 tf of thrust the booster does not get to use.

Against a Saturn V's 3,400 tf, the lost thrust alone is nearly half a Saturn V.

Isp across generations

Vacuum Isp has not moved: 350, 347, 350 s across Raptor 1, 2 and 3. The sea-level figure rose only because the sea-level/vacuum gap closed — nozzle expansion work, not combustion.

02 Payload, every block

Two ceilings — one for a ship that comes home, one for a ship that does not — against the figure SpaceX publishes.

StackHeightGross PropellantRVacT/W ExpendableReusableSpaceX figure RatioFlown

Reusable is the number that matters, and it is not the ceiling. The expendable column spends the whole tank reaching orbit, leaving nothing to land with. Reusable holds back 300 m/s — a deorbit burn plus the landing burn after the flip; entry itself is aerobraking and costs no propellant. That reserve is worth 0 t on Block 1 (which is thrust-bound either way) and 72 t on Block 4.

Neither column is a prediction. With public masses a Block 3 ship arrives in orbit with roughly 345 t of propellant still aboard — it is not propellant-limited. The published 100 t is set by payload-bay volume (614 m³), adapter structure, thermal protection, landing hardware and programme margin. The equation can see none of those, which is why the columns differ from the published figure by a factor of three to eleven.

What it buys

Block 4's ceiling is 659 t — about 31% above Block 3's 503 t, from 26% more total propellant and a taller booster. The gain is real but sublinear: the ship's dry mass grows with the tank, and the extra propellant has to be lifted by the same 33 engines.

Liftoff thrust-to-weight falls to 1.28, the lowest of the four blocks. That is the constraint that will bind first if the ship stretches further.

Six vacuum engines, not three

Going from three RVac to six does not change the delta-v arithmetic — specific impulse is per-engine and the mass flow simply doubles. What it changes is thrust: the ship's vacuum acceleration roughly doubles, which shortens the insertion burn, cuts gravity loss, and gives the vehicle margin to complete the burn on fewer engines if one fails. It also makes the ship a more capable tanker, since refuelling transfers scale with how quickly propellant can be moved.

The 60% propellant increase is the bigger lever. On Block 3 the ship arrives in orbit with roughly 345 t spare; on Block 4, with 2,560 t to start from, that surplus grows to well over 600 t. That is what makes the lunar and Mars architectures plausible — not the payload figure, which stays modest.

05 The model

Two equations, published masses, and an honest account of what they cannot tell you.

  1. Booster burn. Isp_eff·g₀·ln((dry+prop+above)/[dry+above]), where above is the ship and payload — still fully fuelled at hot-staging. Mean Isp is weighted 65/35 between sea level and vacuum, since the engine climbs into thinner air.
  2. Ship burn. Isp_vac·g₀·ln((dry+prop+payload)/(dry+payload)). The vacuum engine's 378 s carries the ship from staging velocity to orbit.
  3. The ceiling, not the answer. Solve for the payload at which the ship exactly spends its tank reaching orbit. Past that the vehicle physically cannot make it. For every block this lands far above the published figure.
  4. Liftoff thrust is the other wall. 33 engines must lift the whole stack at thrust-to-weight of at least 1.20. Below about 1.15 the gravity loss grows faster than extra propellant helps.
  5. What the equation cannot see. Payload-bay volume, adapter structure, thermal protection, flap actuators, landing hardware, boil-off and mission margin. These — not delta-v — set the published numbers.

The weak point is the masses, not the maths. Public dry masses are rounded, non-propellant structure is never itemised, and Block 4's ship mass is an estimate inferred from its length. A ±10% error in ship dry mass moves Block 4's ceiling by roughly ±40 t. Treat these as well-built estimates, not measurements — and note that the ceiling is an upper bound the vehicle could reach, not a figure anyone has flown.
