The Great Pyramid:
how many people?

Mainstream archaeology's numbers — 20 years, 20,000–30,000 workers, 2.3 million blocks — pushed through the arithmetic of copper cutting, copper supply, site preparation and the farming that fed everyone. Move the sliders anywhere — every panel updates.

◇Scenarios

Pick one to load its assumptions into every panel.

01 · Mainstream the position archaeology holds

Every default here is a published figure: 7.5 mm/hr from Stocks' Aswan granite trial, 20 years from Herodotus and Khufu's reign, 120 working days a year because Merer's logbook runs July–November — the Akhet flood, when the fields are under water and the farmers are free anyway. Two-person saw teams, five cut faces per block, one support worker per skilled worker, 12 months of site prep.

This is the page's baseline, and deliberately the reading hardest on its own argument: ≈380,800 people — about 17× the 22,500 headcount, and 25% of Egypt's whole population.

02 · Year-round corvée the most generous reading

Every mainstream value with exactly one change: 300 working days a year instead of 120. No flood downtime, no seasonal rotation, the same crews working almost every day of the build. This single assumption does most of the work in sheltering a 20,000-worker figure, so it is worth isolating — if the mainstream number is reachable at all, it is reachable here.

It buys a 2.5× saving, to ≈153,500. Still ~7× the 22,500 headcount, and just under 10% of the population — but only by pulling farmers off their fields for the entire construction.

03 · Slowest cutting rate the pessimistic floor, still seasonal

Mainstream in every respect except the saw: 2.1 mm/hr, Stocks' wet-sand result on the notched blade, applied to the whole build rather than just the granite that actually needs it. A slower cut per face means more saw-hours, and more saw-hours means more blades being worn away and more men holding them. Labour stays seasonal at 120 days, as in the mainstream case.

The cost lands almost entirely on cutters and their support: ≈1,280,000 people, 3.4× the mainstream result and 83% of the Old Kingdom population.

04 · Harsh slow saw + long reign + year-round

The combination that strips out the three friendly assumptions at once: the 2.1 mm/hr rate, a 23-year span (the top of Khufu's estimated reign, so the calendar stretches as far as the evidence allows), and 300 working days a year. Each of those individually shrinks the workforce. Stacked together they are supposed to make the mainstream figure comfortable.

Even stacked, it lands at ≈443,400 — ~20× the 22,500 headcount, 29% of the population, and only 16% below the plain mainstream case. Shortening the span is what blunts this scenario — the calendar is the one lever the other three cannot beat.

05 · 22,500 — take as long as it takes solve for the calendar

A different question from the other four. They fix the calendar and ask how many people it needs. This one fixes the workforce — the published 22,500, at the mainstream cutting rate, hours, season, support ratio, grain ration and farm yield — and asks how long the pyramid would take. The answer is not a setting, it is a solution: the years slider is placed at the span where the model's total lands on 22,500.

— years. That is — Khufu's longest plausible reign, and — the length of the whole Fourth Dynasty. The crews would be laying one block every —, and the men who set the first courses would be long dead before the capstone went on — roughly — generations of kings to finish one building.

06 · Splitting, not sawing mainstream everywhere, one method swapped

Every assumption identical to Mainstream — 20 years, 9-hour days, 120 working days, the same support ratio, rations and farm yield — with one substitution: the blocks are freed by wedging along bedding planes instead of sawing with copper and sand. A shallow groove or a row of holes is cut along one line, wooden wedges are driven in and wetted, and the block parts along the plane. The split runs the full depth at once, which is exactly why it is cheaper than a saw: one line frees a whole face.

Two numbers here are mine, not published: 1.2 man-hours per metre of groove line, and 2 man-hours per face of dressing afterwards. Nobody has run a controlled splitting trial on Giza limestone, exactly as nobody has run a copper saw on it. Both are set deliberately high so the scenario cannot be accused of being rigged — halve them and the result barely moves, because cutting was never what dominated once the saw was gone.

The total lands at ≈35,200 people — 10.8× below the mainstream case, and just 1.6× the 22,500 headcount rather than 17×. The cutters alone fall from 156,920 to 1,827, an 86-fold cut. But nothing else moves: 4,035 still work the quarry relay, 9,079 the ramps, 14,941 support and 3,408 grow the grain. Splitting removes the saw, not the logistics — and it halves the gap, it does not close it.

Archaeologists generally agree the Great Pyramid was built in about 20 years by roughly 20,000–25,000 people. Those two numbers are quoted everywhere. This page takes them seriously and asks a simple question: what would it actually take?

Start from a measurement rather than a guess. In 1999 Denys Stocks took a 1.8-metre copper saw to a granite quarry at Aswan and set two quarrymen to work it with quartz sand. In fourteen hours they cut a slot 95 cm long and 3 cm deep. That is 7.5 mm an hour — a real figure, from a real experiment, on the hardest stone in the pyramid.

Now multiply it out. 2.3 million blocks, five cut faces each, at that rate. The answer is large enough that you can see the shape of the problem in a single number.

Who were they, according to the mainstream?

Not slaves. That idea comes from Herodotus writing 2,000 years later, and from Hollywood — not from the archaeology. What has actually been excavated is a state-organised workforce that was housed, fed and paid. Lehner's team found barracks flanking bakeries large enough for 1,600–2,000 rotating workers, plus the bakeries to feed them, and crew graffiti naming work gangs. Rations came by role: ten loaves and a measure of beer for an ordinary labourer, more for the skilled. Harvard Magazine · Kemp, ration figures

The workforce was mixed, and that matters for this model. A permanent core of roughly 5,000 lived at Giza year-round — quarrymen, masons, tool-makers, bakers, brewers, scribes and overseers. On top of them came up to 20,000 temporary workers on three-to-four-month shifts, drawn by the corvée: a labour obligation owed to the state, most heavily drawn on farmers during the inundation, when their fields were under water anyway. BBC History — pyramid builders

So the seasonal assumption on this page is not a modelling convenience — it is the documented system. Merer's logbook records his crew arriving by boat in the flood season and being sent elsewhere once the flood ebbed, when hauling became impractical. And the harvest calendar is precisely why farmers are in this model twice: some as the labour that built the pyramid, the rest as the labour that fed it. Archaeology Magazine — Wadi al-Jarf papyri

One thing the mainstream does not claim: that the pyramid was built by an army of slaves. If the arithmetic on this page pushes the workforce into the hundreds of thousands, that is already far outside what the archaeological record describes — and a workforce that size would strain the corvée system itself, because it would mean mass conscription during the one season agriculture cannot spare anyone.

Nothing here is tuned to produce a particular answer. Every default is a published measurement or the mainstream consensus — and where a default pushes against the argument, that is deliberate. Move any slider and the page recomputes honestly; it will happily tell you the mainstream figure is fine if you give it the assumptions that make it so.

The hole you dig first

Before a single block is cut, the site itself is a construction project — and it comes out of the same 20 years, it does not run alongside them.

Khufu did something his predecessors hadn't: he put his pyramid on a base carved into the bedrock, terraced down by what Lehner calls successive approximation — levelling a bit, re-shooting the cardinal alignment, levelling further, checking again. The final surface was a platform of fine Tura limestone slabs about 0.52 m thick, and the north side ended up off true level from the south by less than 1.5 cm. Lehner, JARCE 20 (1983)

Around it, a court was paved in interlocking jigsaw slabs, and the bedrock was cut with corner sockets for the casing, lever sockets for manoeuvring stones, and a long row of post-holes that probably carried the reference lines. AERA's survey has logged over 3,000 of these rock-cut features around the base. AERAGRAM 25.1–2 (AERA)

Cutting the stone

Copper is softer than limestone and far softer than granite. It doesn't cut — it drags quartz sand through the stone, and the sand does the work.

The anchor is Denys Stocks' 1999 field trial at Aswan. He took a 1.8 m copper blade weighing 14.5 kg to a rose-granite quarry and set two Egyptian quarrymen to work it with sand. Dry sand with a flat edge: a 95 cm slot, 3 cm deep, in 14 hours. That's 7.5 mm per hour — and granite is the hard case. Giza's limestone should cut faster. trial summary

Every block needs five faces freed — the base stays attached to bedrock until it's levered out. So one block costs five saw-cuts, each by a two-person crew.

Five things that don't quite fit — in almost every scenario

The arithmetic gives a number. These are the questions the number raises — and what the evidence currently says about each. None of the first five scenarios makes them go away; the numbers in them update with the sliders. The sixth is the exception, and only for the first objection: if nothing is sawn, there is no copper to account for. That is a change of method, not an answer, and the other four objections survive it intact.

01Where is all the copper?

The project needs 1,138 tonnes of copper on the quarry floor at any moment, and wears through about 2,679 t a year replacing it. Blades were reforged rather than thrown away, so the metal circulated — but circulation still needs a standing stock, plus a smelting and reforging industry running continuously beside the work.

The only major Sinai source is Bir Nasib, estimated at about 5,000 tonnes across roughly 1,300 years — an average of ~3.85 tonnes a year, much of it from periods later than Khufu. Annual make-up demand here runs at — that average.

02Does the farmer count fit what we know?

Feeding the workforce takes — farm workers, on the assumptions built into this model. Egypt's population is estimated at about 1.6 million, so that is — of everyone in the country working to feed the pyramid crews.

The question is not whether that is physically possible — it is whether the rest of the economy still functions while — of everyone grows grain for one building. Ancient agriculture was not a high-surplus economy; most output was eaten by the people who grew it.

03Is the total workforce even feasible?

The model wants — people — — of the Old Kingdom population, sustained for — years. The mainstream figure is 20,000–25,000: roughly 4,000 primary labourers and 16,000–20,000 secondary workers, of whom about 5,000 were permanent and up to 20,000 arrived on three-to-four-month shifts.

That is a coherent picture — but notice that it is an archaeological argument from housing and provisioning, not from cutting rates. The workers' camp is sized for that population. There is no known camp for a hundred thousand.

—

04Or was it built with fewer people?

Everything above scales off one number: — mm per hour. That figure comes from granite, which is about 0.1% of the pyramid. If limestone cut two or three times faster — which is plausible, and has never been tested with a copper saw — the cutter count falls by the same factor.

There is also a structural question, and it is no longer hypothetical: whether the block was sawn at all, or split along natural bedding planes with wedges and water. Scenario 06 runs that second method through the same model — five faces per block, one worker instead of two, no blade. It cuts the workforce to 35,224 and the cutters to 1,827, and leaves the copper question with nothing to bite on.

What it does not do is reach 22,500. The 1.6× left over is haulage, ramps, support and grain — the parts of the total that no cutting method touches, and the parts the published headcount leaves out. So the honest reading of this objection is narrower than it looks: a method change is worth a factor of eleven in the quarry, and the last factor of one-and-a-half has to be found somewhere else entirely.

The mainstream model is a permanent core of ~5,000 plus up to 20,000 on three-to-four-month shifts. This model wants — at once, over — working days a year.

If those are rotating shifts of four months, the total number of individuals drawn in is roughly —. Set against a population of 1.6 million that is — — a large share of the able-bodied men, every flood season, for two decades.

First, the mechanism — and why the powder is not simple copper

In the abrasive technique the copper is not the cutting agent, the quartz sand is; the blade only transmits the pressure and is itself abraded in the process. Stocks measured that loss directly: 52 cm³ of copper removed in the same 14 hours that took 170 cm³ of granite — roughly one part copper to three of stone. Spread across this page's own workload that is about — of copper, worn away as metal rather than sawn off in chips.

Freshly cut copper in a wet slit does not stay metallic for long. It oxidises to cuprite (Cu₂O) first, and in wet conditions with iron, arsenic and organic matter present the stable end product is malachite and azurite — which is to say the powder turns into the same green and blue copper minerals the Egyptians were mining in Sinai. Fine grains oxidise almost completely within weeks. So the honest question is not "where is the reddish metal dust", it is "where is the green staining and where is the copper enrichment in the sediment".

And that is what the excavation and the chemistry both report. Drill holes at Saqqara preserve green traces of oxidised copper-and-abrasive pulp on their walls — the slurry, still in place, turned to malachite and cuprite over four and a half millennia. Copper tools from the Giza workers' village have now been analysed and are arsenical copper, not pure copper — harder, and a point that matters below, because arsenic rides along with the copper into the powder and the soil.

The part that does surprise: copper in the harbour mud

The strongest independent check is a geochemical one, and it was published in Geology in 2024. A sediment core from Khufu harbour — the port that received the Tura limestone — shows a copper (and arsenic) enrichment profile that tracks the necropolis's construction in pulses: a pre-dynastic onset around 3,265 BCE, then the big rise through the Fourth Dynasty, aligning with the reigns of Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure. Younes et al., Geology 52 (2024) · Eos summary

This cuts against the absence argument. Copper contamination at Giza is real, dated, and peaks exactly when the pyramid was being built — the authors call it the earliest instance of significant human metal pollution. Whatever the workforce was, it was handling copper at scale, and the plateau still carries the signature.

Note what that signal can and cannot tell us. It is a relative enrichment — a rise against local background — not a count of tonnes, and it was deposited by runoff and harbour traffic rather than weighed at the source. A large-scale tool industry and a workforce of hundreds of thousands both leave a copper signal; the geochemistry cannot separate them, because the copper pours into the same sediment either way.

So what would settle it

A mass balance on the slurry. Every quantity here is either measured (Stocks' wear rate, the 1:3 copper-to-granite ratio) or assumed (how many saw teams worked, for how many days). The wear rate is solid; the total is a multiplication of it, and multiplication is where a factor of a hundred lives. If the copper powder really was produced in the quantities this page's arithmetic implies, the archaeological record ought to contain workshop floors, sumps and spoil heaps with percent-level copper enrichment — and so far what has been reported is baseline-level contamination in sediment and staining on individual worked surfaces, not the mass signature of half a million blades.

Provenance and isotopes. Copper from Bir Nasib carries a distinct lead-isotope and trace-element signature. If Giza's copper contamination derives from that ore being ground to powder on site, its isotopes should match the tools. Differential arsenic-to-copper ratios across the site would also say whether the powder came from tool wear (arsenical copper) or from ore processing.

The experiment nobody has run. Cut Giza limestone — not granite — with a copper saw and quartz sand, capture every gram of debris, and measure the copper fraction. That single number would resolve both this objection and the cutting-rate question at the same time. Stocks, Aswan trials

Honest limits of this section

The wear figures come from two published experiments on granite — Stocks at Aswan, Vasyutin with corundum — and the pyramid is mostly soft limestone, which nobody has tested with a copper saw. The harbour geochemistry measures enrichment, not tonnage, and the sediment signature could equally reflect copper tool production, ore handling, or the copper-alloy casings and fittings alone. And oxidation rates for buried copper powder under Giza conditions are estimated from general corrosion chemistry, not measured on site. This section raises a specific, testable prediction; it does not deliver a verdict on its own.

What this page is actually claiming

Not that the mainstream numbers are wrong, and not that aliens built it. Only this: the published cutting rate, applied to the published block count over the published timeline, does not produce the published workforce. Something in that chain is wrong — the rate, the timeline, the method, or the headcount. Each of those is a real, arguable position, and the sliders let you test which one you would have to give up.

The method is the only one of the four that can be tested without giving anything up: it asks what the stone did, not how hard people worked. Changing it moves the answer by a factor of eleven. Changing the rate by a factor of eleven would mean a stone saw cutting faster than the fastest modern hand saw has ever been measured. That asymmetry is the page's actual finding, and it is why the sixth scenario is the one worth arguing about.

How these sources are graded
  • Peer-reviewed / academic press — journal articles, university press monographs, excavation reports. Treated as authority.
  • Museum & institutional — catalogue records, museum journals, national institutions. Treated as authority for objects and collections.
  • Reference & quality journalism — encyclopedias, Smithsonian, Archaeology, IEEE Spectrum. Used for dates and background, not for contested claims.
  • Primary source — Petrie 1883, excavation reports, the Wadi al-Jarf papyri. Used where the argument turns on what the original observer actually wrote.

No hobbyist sites, advocacy pages or personal blogs are cited. Where a claim originates in a popular book rather than a peer-reviewed study, the page says so at the point of citation — a claim being examined is not the same as a claim being endorsed.

Herodotus states 20 years and reference works still quote it. It also has to fit Khufu's reign, which modern estimates put at c. 23–29 years. Britannica · Khufu's reign

Two-person saw teams

The experimental literature is explicit: two people, one saw, a steady supply of quartz sand. The sand is the cutting agent — copper only transmits the force. Stocks, Aswan trials

Five faces per block

The base is left attached to the bedrock so the block sits on rock rather than loose debris, then levered free last. That is five cut faces, not six.

Where 20,000–30,000 comes from

Lehner and Hawass put peak labour at 20,000–30,000, and the workers' city they excavated could not have housed more than about 20,000. That is an archaeological limit from living space, not an engineering calculation. PBS NOVA — Lehner & Hawass

Seasonal, not year-round

Merer's logbook runs July to November — the Akhet flood, when fields were underwater and farmers were free. NOVA describes a year-round skeleton crew plus a large seasonal surge in exactly those months. The seasonal reading is the better-evidenced one, so the mainstream preset uses 120 working days. Diary of Merer (Wadi al-Jarf papyri) · seasonal surge

The other three scenarios

Year-round corvée — every mainstream value, but 300 working days. This is the assumption that most shelters the 20,000 figure, so it is worth seeing. Slowest cutting rate — Stocks' wet-sand result on hard stone, still seasonal. Harsh — slow rate plus a 23-year span plus year-round corvée. What was removed, and why

The old "maximally generous" preset (20 mm/hr, 40 years, 12-hour days, 360 days) is gone: no source supports those figures together, so it was a foil rather than a scenario. The 10-year build is gone too — it does not fit Khufu's reign and no Egyptologist argues for it.
