The hole you dig first

Before a single block is cut, the site itself is a construction project — and it comes out of the same 20 years, it does not run alongside them.

Khufu did something his predecessors hadn't: he put his pyramid on a base carved into the bedrock, terraced down by what Lehner calls successive approximation — levelling a bit, re-shooting the cardinal alignment, levelling further, checking again. The final surface was a platform of fine Tura limestone slabs about 0.52 m thick, and the north side ended up off true level from the south by less than 1.5 cm.

Around it, a court was paved in interlocking jigsaw slabs, and the bedrock was cut with corner sockets for the casing, lever sockets for manoeuvring stones, and a long row of post-holes that probably carried the reference lines. AERA's survey has logged over 3,000 of these rock-cut features around the base.

Cutting the stone

Copper is softer than limestone and far softer than granite. It doesn't cut — it drags quartz sand through the stone, and the sand does the work.

The anchor is Denys Stocks' 1999 field trial at Aswan. He took a 1.8 m copper blade weighing 14.5 kg to a rose-granite quarry and set two Egyptian quarrymen to work it with sand. Dry sand with a flat edge: a 95 cm slot, 3 cm deep, in 14 hours. That's 7.5 mm per hour — and granite is the hard case. Giza's limestone should cut faster.

Every block needs five faces freed — the base stays attached to bedrock until it's levered out. So one block costs five saw-cuts, each by a two-person crew.

The copper bill

Copper doesn't last. It grinds away into the slurry, the blade thins, and eventually it has to be reforged.

Stocks measured the loss: 52 cm³ of copper gone in that 14-hour cut, against 170 cm³ of granite removed. An independent replication by Vasyutin, using corundum abrasive, found a copper-to-granite volume ratio of 1 : 5.8. Different experiment, same order of magnitude.

Saw lifespan

—

A 14.5 kg blade is about 1,620 cm³ of copper. Allow it to lose 20% and it is too thin to hold a straight cut. At 3.7 cm³ burned per hour, that is the number above.

Moving the stone

Cutters don't carry. The delivery rate is fixed by the pyramid's volume divided by the available days — it doesn't care how many people you have.

The main quarry sits about 500 m south of the pyramid, so most blocks travel a short distance by sledge, then go up a ramp. Each is a separate crew working a relay: a block leaves the quarry every few minutes, for years, and nothing about adding workers makes that faster.

The pace

—

Feeding everyone

Every worker eats. In a grain economy that means farmers — and the farmers don't appear in anyone's pyramid workforce estimate, because they never left their fields.

Ramesside basin land yielded about 2,700–2,900 litres per hectare. One man working roughly a hectare produced about 3,000 litres. Administrative records describe an ideal where a single cultivator's output fed four nuclear families — so one farm worker feeding several people is the documented best case, not a fantasy.
