The Stone Vases

Before the pyramids, Egypt was already mass-producing vessels from granite, diorite and basalt — stone hard enough to blunt steel. Tens of thousands survive. This page asks who made them, how long each one took, and why a handful of them are too precise to explain.

Egypt produced tens of thousands of stone vessels before the first pyramid was finished — not pottery, but granite, diorite, basalt, porphyry, rock crystal. More than 40,000 were sealed in the galleries beneath Djoser's Step Pyramid alone.

Archaeology's answer is not in dispute: they were made by hand, with flint borers, copper tubes and quartz sand, ground and drilled over days or weeks each. That answer is well-evidenced and experimental work has reproduced it.

So this page does two things. First it takes the hand-made explanation at its word and asks what it costs: how long is one vase, and how many people is the whole corpus? Then it looks hard at the one thing that does not fit — a handful of vessels measured to thousandths of an inch, and what that measurement does and does not prove.

Roughly half of what follows is about that precision, because it is the only part of this story where the numbers are genuinely contested. Where the evidence is strong I say so. Where it is not, I say that too.

The anchor measurement

Denys Stocks did not guess. In his experimental programme he made vessels the ancient way, start to finish, and timed himself.

22.5 hours — a complete limestone vase, drilled and bored to a finished interior: 8 cm maximum internal diameter, 5.5 cm minimum, a 4.5 cm mouth and 10 cm depth. Every stage documented, every tool reconstructed from tomb reliefs. Stocks, Experiments in Egyptian Archaeology (2003)

And the hard-stone penalty is brutal. With a bow-driven copper tube in sand, Stocks cut limestone at 6 cm³/hour. In granite and diorite the same tube managed 2 cm³/hour, and the twist-reverse-twist rig only 0.4 cm³/hour — a 15× penalty for the hardest stones. That is the whole reason most surviving vases are not granite.

Now scale that — and here the arithmetic goes somewhere unexpected. Forty thousand vessels at those rates is roughly 2 million person-hours. Spread over the century or so in which they were amassed, that is about ten craftsmen.

So the hand-made story is affordable. That is not the weak point, and anyone arguing against the mainstream should stop pretending otherwise. A few dozen skilled people can account for the entire corpus, and the excavated workshop evidence fits that perfectly.

The problem is not how many. It is how straight — and that is where the rest of this page goes.

Carving one vase

A hard-stone vessel is not carved from the outside in. It is hollowed through a hole narrower than the cavity behind it — the drill enters a 4.5 cm mouth and has to open an 8 cm chamber inside. You cannot see what you are cutting.

The sequence, per Stocks

1
Rough out the exterior. Stone hand-axes and pounders shape the block. Flint is harder than granite and will cut it, slowly.
2
Drill the mouth. A copper tube spun with a bow, fed with quartz sand. The sand does the cutting; the copper only carries it.
3
Hollow the body. Successive tubes of increasing size, then figure-of-eight borers worked by hand. This is where the hours go.
4
Remove the cores. The drilled cylinders are snapped out with a chisel.
5
Smooth the cusps. Each borer leaves a raised ridge inside. These are ground away with hand-held sandstone rubbers through the narrow mouth.
6
Polish. The final surface, inside and out, worked down to the finish you see in a museum case.
What this costs the worker

Stocks is blunt about the physical toll: years of this work caused repetitive strain injury in the hands, wrists and forearms, and the constant fine dust meant vessel drillers probably died young. This was not a prestige craft at the bench. It was a sentence.

Precision

Everything above assumes hand work. It assumes the vase is ground true by eye and by feel, because that is what a hand worker can do. Then the vases were measured.

Since 2023, a number of hard-stone vessels have been put through industrial metrology — structured-light and CT scanning, analysed to tolerances of thousandths of an inch. The results are the most interesting thing in this entire subject.

What was measured

Circularity
≤ 0.001"
how round a cross-section is
Concentricity
≤ 0.001"
whether successive rings share a centre
Coaxiality
≤ 0.001"
inner and outer axes aligned

Those are Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing metrics — the standards used to inspect machined parts in a factory. Applying them to a 5,000-year-old granite jar is odd. Finding that the jar scores well on them is odder.

Why that is hard to explain

▸Hand grinding is asymmetric by nature

A hand pushes harder on one side than the other. It wanders. It cannot hold an axis across thousands of rotations while the stone wears away underneath it — and the tool wears too, so the geometry drifts as you go. A cross-section ground by hand is usually round-ish. A thousandth of an inch is 25 microns — about a quarter the width of a human hair.

▸Inner and outer surfaces agree

Coaxiality is the awkward one. The inside is made through the mouth, by different tools, at a different time, by a different hand from whoever roughed the outside. For the two axes to line up to a thousandth of an inch implies both surfaces were referenced to the same fixed centre — which is what a lathe does, and what hand work has no mechanism to do.

▸Lugs and handles align to the axis

The handle lugs sit with machine-like symmetry to the vessel's rotational axis. Glyphs cut by hand elsewhere in Egypt show exactly the human variation you would expect. These do not.

Put a thousandth of an inch in perspective

0.001"= 25 µm. About a quarter of the width of a human hair, or a fifth of a grain of table salt. This is the tolerance being reported on a granite jar.
0.01"= 254 µm. Roughly the thickness of a piece of paper. Even at this looser number, a hand-ground interior would be remarkable inside an 8 cm chamber reached through a 4.5 cm hole.
0.1"= 2.5 mm. What you would naively expect from skilled hand grinding at this scale. The reported measurements are 100× tighter than this, which is the whole puzzle in one line.

The signature argument, in plain terms

The most useful thing in the 2025 study is that it does not just report a number — it reads a pattern. Different ways of making a vessel fail differently:

If hand-ground

The outside, worked with the vessel braced against the body, tends to come out reasonably concentric — you are rotating it by hand and it stays roughly on centre. But it is rarely truly circular, because the pressure is uneven. Inside, the borers wander: poor concentricity. So you predict good concentricity / poor circularity outside, and the reverse inside.

If lathe-turned

The workpiece is fixtured on a fixed axis, so everything is referenced to it: circularity, concentricity and coaxiality all come out excellent together. You do not get one good and one bad — a lathe cannot produce that combination.

Fomitchev-Zamilov found the first signature on the 19 provenanced Petrie vessels. That is a strong, specific result — not a shrug about "hand tools are good enough", but a testable prediction that came out the way the hand-made model requires.

The honest complication — and it is large

Nearly all the most impressive scanned vases are unprovenanced. They were bought on the antiquities market, without excavation context. For a lot of archaeologists that means their date — and possibly their Egyptian origin — cannot be established at all. Meanwhile Egyptian craft workshops today demonstrably make hard-stone replicas good enough to fool authentication. A precision measurement on an object of unknown date is not yet a claim about ancient Egypt. This is the single biggest weakness in the case, and it is not a small one.

And then a peer-reviewed study pushed back

In 2025 Max Fomitchev-Zamilov published a metrological method in npj Heritage Science (Nature portfolio). He scanned 19 provenanced Predynastic vessels from the Petrie Museum against 25 modern machine-made and 24 contemporary handcrafted comparators, and classified them statistically.

The result cut against the machining claim: the ancient vessels form a distinctive signature — good outer concentricity with poor circularity, and the reverse inside — which matches hand grinding and boring, not lathe turning. Two headline unprovenanced vases clustered with the modern machine-made comparators.

Notably, Fomitchev-Zamilov had earlier published measurements sympathetic to the precision claims and publicly retracted them after expanding his sample. That is the scientific process working, and it is worth weighing.

The debate, stated fairly

This is a genuinely live argument, and both sides have real points. Here is the strongest version of each.

The mainstream position

  • Tens of thousands of provenanced vessels anchor the industry to c. 4000–2800 BC, many inscribed with early royal names.
  • Unfinished vessels showing every production stage exist from excavated workshops — we can see the method, not just the result.
  • Stocks' experiments reproduce the work with flint, copper, sand and slow rotation.
  • Surviving drill cores with spiral striations prove tube drilling was real.
  • The 2025 npj study found provenanced vessels match hand grinding.
  • Modern Egyptian workshops produce convincing hard-stone replicas.

The precision case

  • Tolerances of a few thousandths of an inch on granite, measured by industrial metrology.
  • Inner and outer axes coaxial to within 25 µm — hard to achieve without a fixed centre.
  • Lug symmetry aligned to the rotational axis.
  • Recurring geometric proportions (pi, phi) claimed across independently scanned vessels.
  • Some carry collection histories reaching back to the 1960s, before precision forgery seems plausible.
  • Precision also appears in provenanced dynastic contexts — drill cores, sawn basalt, the Serapeum boxes — so it cannot simply be quarantined as a private-collection artefact.
Where the argument actually stands

Both sides agree on more than the noise suggests. Nobody serious claims aliens. Nobody serious claims every vase is a fake. The real open questions are narrow and testable: will the great provenanced collections in Cairo, Turin and Boston submit their finest vessels to the same metrology? Can authentication science reliably separate ancient from modern? And has anyone fully replicated a headline vase, start to finish, on the record? Until those are answered, the honest position is that the precision is real and measured, and its explanation is unsettled — with provenance as the weak link on the extraordinary claim.

04What would settle it?

Three things, all achievable: scan the finest provenanced vessels in Cairo, Turin and Boston; run a blind authentication study on the private-collection pieces; and attempt a documented full replication of a headline vase to its measured tolerance. None of these has been done. That is why this page ends in a question rather than a verdict.

The precision is not in doubt. Its explanation is.

What this page is actually claiming

That the mainstream hand-made account is well-evidenced and experimentally reproduced — and that it is also expensive, in a way rarely stated out loud. And that a small number of vessels carry measurements which no published experiment has yet matched by hand, on objects whose provenance does not let us date them confidently. Both of those sentences are true at the same time, and the interesting question is which one gives way.
