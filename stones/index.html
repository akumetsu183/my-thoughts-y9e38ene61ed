Megaliths · measured

There is a stone in Lebanon that no machine has ever lifted. It weighs 1,650 tonnes.

It has sat in the same quarry for two thousand years, fully cut, still attached to the bedrock it was carved from. Nobody moved it. Every other giant stone you have heard of is smaller than that one — and the record for what we can lift today is not a stone at all.

Forgotten Stone, Baalbek — 19.6 m × 6 m × ≥5.5 m. The orange tick beside it is a 1.8 m person, drawn to the same scale.

03 — Three different words

Quarried ≠ moved ≠ lifted

Almost every "ancient giants" article puts the biggest cut stone next to the biggest lift and implies they are the same problem. They are not. Cutting a stone loose is a question of labour and patience. Moving it is a question of friction. Lifting it — getting mass off the ground and holding it there — is the one the modern world actually solved.

Look at the maximum in each column, honestly:

Cut, never moved
1,650
tonnes · Baalbek, Roman

The Forgotten Stone. Fully detached on three sides, resting on its own bedrock. If it had been finished and moved it would be the heaviest object ever relocated by hand. It wasn't. This is the ceiling of the "how big could they cut" question, not the "what did they move" question.

Actually moved
1,250
tonnes · St Petersburg, 1770

The Thunder Stone — moved 6 km over frozen ground on bronze balls, and cut down from 1,500 t on the way. The heaviest ancient stone genuinely moved is the Ramesseum colossus at 1,000 t, 220 km. Both were dragged. Neither was ever lifted.

05 — The ones that travelled

What it costs to move a thousand tonnes

The honest answer is: it costs friction and time, not mystery. Three cases worth knowing, because each breaks a different assumption.

Baalbek, three stones of ~800 t. Moved a few hundred metres along earthen banks, downhill, on rollers. Downhill is the entire trick. This is the largest logged ancient move of a squared block, and the sources are unusually open about the mechanism. Baalbek Stones

Colossi of Memnon, 720 t each. Quarried near modern Cairo and taken 675 km overland to Thebes — because the stones were considered too heavy to take upstream on the Nile. That claim is worth sitting with: they chose a 675 km desert haul over a river barge. Colossi of Memnon

The Ramesseum colossus, ~1,000 t. The largest statue ever moved in Egypt, transported roughly 220 km from Aswan. It is now a torso and a base. Ramesseum

And the counter-example that proves the rule: the Thunder Stone. 1770, not ancient — but it is the cleanest test of the "just drag it" method, because it was documented. ~1,500 t cut down to 1,250 t, moved 6 km on bronze spheres rolling along a track, in winter, on frozen ground, with about 400 men over nine months. Every part of that recipe is about removing friction. Guinness

None of the four above involved a crane capable of more than ~3 tonnes. The Romans' best treadwheel crane had a working ceiling around 6 tonnes; four men on a winch managed 3. Every one of these moves is ground-level friction engineering.

06 — The inversion

This is the chart that actually surprises people

Read that again, but in the right order. The ancient world moved stones far heavier than it ever lifted. That's not a paradox — it's a direct consequence of how levers, rollers and ramps work. Getting mass up onto a wall is cheap if you can build a slope beside it. Getting mass straight up is where you need either an enormous counterweight or an enormous engine.

The Romans knew this and built around it. Baalbek's Jupiter temple has an architrave block of roughly 163 t placed at ~19 m — high, yes, but reached with a construction ramp and a crane that was never asked to do more than a fraction of that at once. Everything ancient and tall is built by stacking, not by hoisting.

Brihadisvara temple in Thanjavur, 1010 CE, set an 80 t granite block at about 66 m. That is the highest heavy stone placement before the industrial era, and it is still only 80 tonnes — because it too was built up from the ground, one course at a time.

07 — What we can do now

And here is the honest modern ledger

Four records, and the important thing about each is what kind of machine it needed.

Heaviest single crane lift
20,133
tonnes · Taisun, Yantai, 2008

A gantry crane with a 20,000 t safe working load, spanning a shipyard dry dock. The record lift was a barge ballasted with water. It has never lifted a comparable mass of stone, and nobody has needed it to. It exists to drop 20,000 t ships into the sea.

Heaviest lift at sea
17,000
tonnes · Sleipnir, North Sea, 2022

Two 10,000 t revolving cranes working in tandem on a semi-submersible. The load was a gas process module, 47 m tall, run as a single pick — the heaviest crane lift ever made afloat. Notice the pattern: the record is always an object made to be lifted.

Largest land crane
5,000
tonnes · SGC-250 "Big Carl"

A ring crane with a 250,000 tonne-metre load moment, reaching 250 m. At Hinkley Point C it made 700+ lifts over four years; the heaviest components were around 1,600 t. That is still a sixth of the Forgotten Stone.

Heaviest land transport
23,000
tonnes · Fagioli, 2024

An offshore wind installation vessel moved on 880 axle lines of self-propelled modular transporter. Not lifted — rolled. The modern transport record is 14× the ancient move record, and it needs 3,520 wheels to do it.

For scale on the small end: the largest helicopter ever built lifts 20 t on a sling. The heaviest statue move of the 21st century — Michael Heizer's Levitated Mass — was a 340 t boulder over 170 km, on a purpose-built transporter, in 2012. The modern world's biggest stone move is a fifth of the Thunder Stone, and needed a custom vehicle.

08 — The bit that cuts both ways

We are not actually better at this than they were. We are just more powerful.

It would be easy, and wrong, to end this as "modern engineering wins." Look at what the numbers actually say:

01

No one alive has lifted 1,650 tonnes of stone. The heaviest crane lift ever is 20,133 t of barge; the largest stone ever lifted into place by modern machinery is in the tens of tonnes. Our records are all in steel, ships and concrete — materials engineered to be lifted. Stone is not.

02

The ancient ceiling on lifting is genuinely low. 230 t for the Theodoric roof slab, ~6 t for a working Roman crane. Whatever moved the 800 t Baalbek blocks did not lift them — the quarry being uphill is the whole story, and anyone who tells you otherwise is skipping the sentence.

03

The ancient ceiling on moving is genuinely absurd. 720 t over 675 km of desert. 1,000 t of statue over 220 km. These are not lifting problems; they're logistics problems solved with rope, sledges, water, ramps and a workforce we can now quantify to within an order of magnitude.

04

The number that should bug you is 19.6 metres. Not the weight. The Forgotten Stone was cut with three faces freed and a base still attached to the mountain, all square, all true, with a tolerance we could not hit on a block that size today without a far bigger machine. The mass is explainable. The precision on a 1,650 t stone is the actual open question.

05

And the honest correction: the "how many men" question is the weakest part of this whole debate, and it is why this page leads with mass instead. Men-per-tonne estimates range from 1 to 30 depending on the ground, the sledge and who ran the experiment. Mass is measured. Workforce is guessed. When your argument rests on a guessed figure, your argument is a guess.

09 — Verdict

So — how big are they, really?

Enormous, and smaller than the stories. Here is the page's whole argument in five lines.

→

1,650 tonnes is the biggest cut stone. It never moved and it never will. Treat it as an engineering ambition, not a completed feat.

→

1,250 tonnes is the heaviest stone ever moved. And it was moved in 1770, with bronze balls and frozen ground. Ancient Egypt's own record, the Ramesseum colossus, is 1,000 t.

→

230 tonnes is the heaviest stone ever lifted, until the industrial era. Modern cranes beat that by roughly 90×, and they do it hundreds of times per project.

→

The gap is in lifting, not in moving. On the ground, ancient engineers were within an order of magnitude of what we do. In the air, they were 6 tonnes and we are 20,133.

→

Which leaves the real question exactly where it was: not "how did they lift it" — they mostly didn't — but how did they cut it that precisely, and how did they keep it together over 675 km? That's the one the ladder doesn't answer.

Method: every mass on this page is a published estimate, and they vary by up to ±15% because sandstone and granite density differ.
